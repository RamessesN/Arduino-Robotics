\textit{TCRT5000} is the most important IR sensor in this experiment as shown in \fref{fig:TCRT5000}.
It mainly includes an IR Tx (transmitter) and IR Rx (receiver),
the former of which is a Photodiode while the latter is a Phototransistor.\cite{TCRT5000L_datasheet}
It is capable of detecting changes in light intensity.
The infrared-emitting diode of this device continuously emits infrared light.
When the emitted infrared light is not reflected or the reflected intensity is insufficient,
the phototransistor remains in the cut-off state.
The module's output terminal is at a low voltage level, and the indicator diode stays off.
When an object to be detected enters the detection range,
the infrared light is reflected back with sufficient intensity.
The phototransistor then enters the saturation state,
its module's output terminal switches to a high voltage level, and
the indicator diode is illuminated.

\subsection{Method}

The IR sensor voltage coming back was measured as
$4.15 \pm 0.1 \, \text{V}$ on black lines, and $0.15 \pm 0.1\,\text{V}$ on white ground.
We observed a
We found a linear transition region that in the middle of black and white surfaces,
enabling analog position detection.
Therefore, we realized that it can be applied under PID control.

The linear voltage gradient permits error quantification for PID control.
PID control is a combined method of proportional, integral and derivative control.
To implement PID control, there should be a standard, standing for adjustment goal.
The most recent value should be measured and then calculate the \textit{error} between them.
Simply proportional adjustment is the quickest and easiest way,
which is just a proportional coefficient (\(Kp\)) times error,
described as \eref{eq:p-control}.

\begin{equation}
  P = Kp * error
  \label{eq:p-control}
\end{equation}

Integral control is similar.
Its difference is that each time the integral coefficient (\(Ki\)) times
the total sum of all errors calculated in the history,
described as \eref{eq:i-control}.

\begin{equation}
  I = Ki * \int_0^t error * dt
  \label{eq:i-control}
\end{equation}

And derivative control, as its name suggests, is
a derivative coefficient (\(Kd\)) times the deviation between this error and the last error.
It can be described as \eref{eq:d-control1} where dt refers to the time offset.

\begin{equation}
  D = Kd * \frac{error}{dt}
  \label{eq:d-control1}
\end{equation}

The total PID control is all three adjustments added together.
All equations above are described mathematically, but are challenging for computers to understand.
In coding environment, they need to be changed \textit{discretely},
which, in total, can be described as \eref{eq:pid-control}, where
\(e(k)\)= position error related to time \(t\).
In car situations, PD control is better than full PID control because
integral term accumulates historical error causes overshoot.

\begin{equation}
  \text{PID Output} = K_p e(k) + K_i \sum_{\tau=0}^k e(\tau) + K_d (e(k) - e(k-1))
  \label{eq:pid-control}
\end{equation}

**3. Sensor Array Configuration**

**4. Line-Loss Recovery Protocol**

1. Recall last deviation direction (e.g., "right-trending")
2. Execute in-place rotation toward memorized direction
3. Terminate when any sensor detects black line
