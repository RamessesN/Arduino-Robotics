\section{Light Seeking}
\label{sec:light-seeking}

\subsection{Theory and Technical Background}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Figures/LDR}
  \rule{35em}{0.5pt}
  \caption{
    Photoresistors. \\
    Resource: \href{https://makeabilitylab.github.io/physcomp/sensors/assets/images/Photoresistors\_Wikipedia.png}{makeabilitylab.github.io/physcomp}
  }
  \label{fig:ldr}
\end{figure}

The operating principle of \gls{ldr}s is rooted in
the photoelectric properties of semiconductor compounds.
When exposed to light, materials like cadmium sulfide (CdS) undergo photon absorption
that generates electron-hole pairs within their crystalline structure.
As illumination intensifies,
this photogeneration effect produces a greater number of charge carriers, thereby
enhancing the material's conductivity.
Consequently, \gls{ldr}s exhibit an inverse relationship between incident light intensity and
electrical resistance-a characteristic that makes them valuable for light-sensing applications.
This nonlinear response proves particularly sensitive in the 10--100 lux range, where
a 20\% increase in light intensity causes approximately 60\% resistance drop.

\begin{figure}[h!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{Figures/i-r_curve}
  \rule{35em}{0.5pt}
  \caption{
    LDRs' Intensity-Resistance curves. \\
    Resource: \href{https://revise.im/content/02-physics/01-unit-1/03-current-electricity/ldr.jpg}{revise.im}
  }
  \label{fig:i-r-curve}
\end{figure}

\gls{ldr}s in our system demonstrate a measurable resistance range
from $1\,\text{k}\Omega$ under direct sunlight to $1.5\,\text{M}\Omega$ in complete darkness,
as recorded by our multimeter.

We use two DC gearbox motors (TT motors) to control \gls{car}'s movement, 
which typically requires \gls{pwm} for speed regulation.

The three key parameters of \gls{pwm} control are frequency, duty cycle, and resolution, 
defined as follows:

\begin{equation}
  \begin{cases}
    \text{Freq} = \frac{1}{T_S} \\
    \text{Duty} = \frac{T_{\text{ON}}}{T_S} \\
    \text{Reso} = \frac{1}{T_S}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $Freq$, $Duty$, and $Reso$ represent the frequency, duty cycle, and resolution of the PWM signal,
$T_S$ is the total period of one \gls{pwm} cycle, and
$T_ON$ is the high-level (active) time within the cycle.

The duty cycle is the most critical parameter. 
For example, a 50\% duty cycle corresponds to approximately half of the motor's full speed, while
a 100\% duty cycle drives the motor at maximum speed.

These characteristics directly influence \gls{car}'s light-seeking behavior.

\subsection{Method}

With $Arduino$'s help, gls{pwm} control can be simply done by setting motor's voltage.
In package, it is $0$ to $255$.

Two \gls{ldr}s were mounted on the left and right front sides of \gls{car},
each returning a sensor reading between 0 and 1023.
Then, the \gls{ldr} values were scaled down by a factor of 4 to match the motor input range.
\gls{car}’s steering direction was determined by the difference in illumination between the two \gls{ldr}s:
if the left-side reading was lower than the right, the right motor’s voltage was reduced (and vice versa),
causing \gls{car} to turn toward the brighter side.
Additionally, if both sensors detected insufficient light, set to over $1019$,
the motors would stop entirely, preventing movement in darkness.
It can be described in pseudocode as follows:

\begin{minted}{cpp}
if(Left < 1019 || Right < 1019) {
  if(Gap >= expect_Gap) {
    if(Left < Right) {
      turnLeft();
    } else {
      turnRight();
    }
  } else {
    goStraight();
  }
} else {
  stop();
}
\end{minted}

\subsection{Results}

In the end, we managed to set straight speed at $120$.
\gls{car} passed the test twice.
On the first attempt, its turning speed was set too fast,
which caused uncontrollable swaying, and our teacher was not satisfied with that performance.
On the second attempt, it performed very well, 
scoring 29 out of 30, losing 1 point for requiring two attempts.

\section{Line Following}
\label{sec:line-following}

\subsection{Theory and Technical Background}

Shown in \fref{fig:TCRT5000}, \textit{TCRT5000} serves as the cornerstone in this experiment.
This reflective optoelectronic device integrates
both an infrared emitter (950nm photodiode) and receiver (NPN phototransistor) in a compact 4-pin package,
distinguished by its blue emitter and black receiver components.
\cite{TCRT5000L_datasheet}

During operation, the photodiode transmits modulated infrared signals while
the phototransistor detects reflected radiation.
The sensor's daylight-blocking optical filter ensures reliable operation under ambient lighting conditions.

\subsection{Method}

The \gls{ir} sensor voltage coming back was measured as
$4.15 \pm 0.1 \, \text{V}$ on black lines, and $0.15 \pm 0.1\,\text{V}$ on white ground.
We observed a linear transition region that in the middle of black and white surfaces,
enabling analog position detection suitable for \gls{pid} control.

The linear voltage gradient permits error quantification for PID control.
\gls{pid} combines proportional (P), integral (I) and derivative (D) components.
To implement \gls{pid} control,
there should be a reference value (setpoint) as the control target, standing for adjustment goal.
Continuous measurement of the process variable to get the most recent value and then
calculate the \textit{error} between the setpoint and this value.

Proportional control (P) applies a correction proportional to the current error.
Simply proportional adjustment is the quickest and easiest way,
which is just a proportional coefficient (\(Kp\)) times error,
described as \eref{eq:p-control}.

\begin{equation}
  P = Kp \cdot error
  \label{eq:p-control}
\end{equation}

Integral control (I) is similar; it accounts for accumulated past errors.
Its difference is that each time the integral coefficient (\(Ki\)) times
the total sum of all errors calculated in the history,
described as \eref{eq:i-control}.

\begin{equation}
  I = Ki \cdot \int_0^t error \cdot dt
  \label{eq:i-control}
\end{equation}

And derivative control (D), as its name suggests, is
a derivative coefficient (\(Kd\)) times the deviation between this error and the last error.
It responds to the error rate of change,
described as \eref{eq:d-control1} where dt refers to the time offset.

\begin{equation}
  D = Kd \cdot \frac{error}{dt}
  \label{eq:d-control1}
\end{equation}

The total \gls{pid} control is all three adjustments added together.
All equations above are described mathematically, but are challenging for computers to understand.
In coding environment, they need to be changed \textit{discretely},
which, in total, can be described as \eref{eq:pid-control}, where
\(e(k)\)= position error related to time \(t\).
In car situations, PD control is better than full \gls{pid} control because
integral term accumulates historical error causes overshoot.

\begin{equation}
  u(k) = K_p e(k) + K_i \sum_{\tau=0}^k e(\tau) + K_d (e(k) - e(k-1))
  \label{eq:pid-control}
\end{equation}

With only \gls{pid} control, \gls{car} is still easy to veere off-track.
We also applied a not-found-line function to overcome this issue.
The function keeps recalling the last direction of deviation, and
once \gls{car} loses its direction,
it keeps rotating toward the memorized direction until any sensor detects a black line.

On \gls{car}, three TCRT5000 \gls{ir} sensors are mounted in parallel with a 1.2 cm inter-sensor spacing.
The voltage response of a single sensor exhibits a piecewise-linear characteristic,
combining two distinct linear segments.
Therefore, we construct a voltage-position graph \fref{fig:v-x-graph} to analyze the sensor behavior.

The Left sensor's voltage minus the right yields a short linear segment around the origin $o$.
The middle sensor's data verifies the peak values detected by the left/right sensors.
Once the middle sensor cannot see black lines,
we mirror the outermost sensor data to artificially extend the linear region.

\section{Obstacle Avoidance}
\label{sec:obstacle-avoidance}

\subsection{Theory and Technical Background}

In this experiment, \textit{HC-SR04} and \textit{SG90} are the two main sensors,
showed in \fref{fig:hcsr04-sg90}.

\begin{figure}[h!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{Figures/HC-SR04_SG90}
  \rule{35em}{0.5pt}
  \caption[HC-SR04 and SG90]{
    HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor and SG90 servo motor diagram;
    pins of HC-SR04 are arranged from left to right as:
    \textit{VCC}, \textit{Trig}, \textit{Echo} and \textit{GND}.
  }
  \label{fig:hcsr04-sg90}
\end{figure}

The HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor mainly consists of a transmitter and a receiver.
This sensor has a measurement range of 2\,cm to 400\,cm with an accuracy of 0.3\,cm.
\cite{HC_SR04_UserGuide}
Two pins are particularly important:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textit{Trig pin} serves as a trigger.
    Only a single high-level pulse needs to be applied to this pin during each measurement cycle.
  \item The \textit{Echo pin} outputs a high-level signal when the HC-SR04 emits ultrasonic waves.
    This signal remains high until the sensor receives the reflected echo.
\end{itemize}

The HC-SR04 transmitter emits ultrasonic waves in one direction at a time.
When these waves encounter an obstacle, they are reflected back and detected by the receiver.
The distance between the HC-SR04 and the obstacle can be calculated
by measuring the time difference between the emission and reception of the ultrasonic wave,
combined with the speed of sound, as described in \eref{distance}:

\begin{equation}
  d = \frac{v \cdot \Delta t}{2}
  \label{distance}
\end{equation}

where $d$ is the distance, $v$ is the speed of the ultrasonic wave, and $\Delta t$ is the time difference.

During this process, the Echo pin's signal changes as follows:
\begin{itemize}
  \item High level when the ultrasonic wave is emitted;
  \item Low level when the reflected wave is received.
\end{itemize}

The SG90 servo is a angle servo driver, suitable for control systems
that require continuous angle changes and the ability to maintain a specific angle.

\subsection{Method}

We designed a \texttt{carDrive} function for obstacle avoidance using a finite state machine. 
It processes sensor inputs and executes sequenced maneuvers when obstacles are detected.
The complete logic of this function is shown in \fref{fig:mermaid}.

\textbf{Normal Operation}

In practice, \gls{car} may encounter obstacles from multiple directions,
primarily from the left, front, and right.
We mounted an SG90 servo motor on \gls{car}'s front, with an HC-SR04 ultrasonic sensor attached.
This allows the sensor to rotate and scan a wider area.

\begin{figure}[h!tbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{Figures/safe_space}
  \rule{35em}{0.5pt}
  \caption[Safety Boundary Visualization]{
    A rectangular Coordinate of the collision risk assessment model, shows fused sensor coverage (shaded).
  }
  \label{fig:safe-space}
\end{figure}

To ensure comprehensive 180$^\circ$ obstacle detection, we implemented a sensor fusion method combining frontal ultrasonic ranging (40\,cm threshold) and lateral infrared proximity sensing (25\,cm threshold). \fref{fig:safe-space} demonstrates the resulting safety envelope.

The SG90 servo rotates in 90-degree increments,
performing a scanning motion at each position.
Three distance measurements are obtained by:
Front distance directly triggers avoidance maneuvers;
Left and right distances are processed using \eref{eq:safe-dist}:

\begin{equation}
  \text{sideSafeDist} = \frac{\text{sideDist} \cdot \text{frontDist}}{\sqrt{\text{sideDist}^2 + \text{frontDist}^2}}
  \label{eq:safe-dist}
\end{equation}

where:
\textit{sideSafeDist} is the effective clearance distance,
\textit{sideDist} represents left or right sensor readings, and
\textit{frontDist} is the front sensor reading

The vehicle moves straight when all clearance distances are safe.
If any calculated distance falls below the threshold,
the system transitions to avoidance mode.

\textbf{Avoidance Maneuvers}

\textbf{\texttt{STEP\_BACK}}: 
First, reverse to create maneuvering space. 
Then check if sufficient clearance exists for a right turn 
(our designated avoidance direction). 
If adequate space exists, transition to \texttt{STEP\_TURN\_RIGHT\_90}. 
Otherwise, proceed to \texttt{STEP\_BACK\_EXTRA}.
        
\textbf{\texttt{STEP\_BACK\_EXTRA}}: 
Perform additional reverse movement. 
Then transition to \texttt{STEP\_TURN\_RIGHT\_90}.
        
\textbf{\texttt{STEP\_TURN\_RIGHT\_90}}: 
Execute 90-degree right turn. 
Then proceed to \texttt{STEP\_DONE}.
        
\textbf{\texttt{STEP\_DONE}}: 
Complete full stop and restart the control loop.

\subsection{Results}

The safety thresholds were empirically determined through iterative testing. The side clearance distance was set to 25\,cm to account for gradual distance reduction during lateral approach, while the frontal clearance used a more conservative 40\,cm threshold to ensure adequate reaction time for head-on obstacles. For turning maneuvers, a minimum 40\,cm clearance was required to execute 90° right turns safely.

Motion timing parameters were carefully calibrated, with each reverse segment lasting 100\,ms and right turns completing in 300\,ms, optimized for the gearbox motor's response characteristics. In final testing within a 3\,m $\times$ 3\,m arena, the \gls{car} demonstrated consistent performance.

\section{Environment Sensing}
\label{sec:environment-sensing}

\subsection{Theory and Technical Background}

In some situations, a robotics car needs to measure humidity, atmospheric pressure, and temperature.
Additionally, it needs to change its speed due to a single direction's sudden linear acceleration.
Therefore, a BME280 sensor is used to overcome environment sensing problem, and
an MPU6050 sensor is used to measure linear acceleration.
The two sensors are as \fref{fig:BME280-MPU6050} shows.

A \textbf{BME280} is a temperature humidity and pressure sensor, which
reads barometric pressure, temperature, and humidity.
Because pressure changes with altitude, you can also estimate altitude.
The BME280 sensor uses \gls{iic} or \gls{spi} communication protocol to exchange data with a microcontroller.
More details here: \href{https://randomnerdtutorials.com/bme280-sensor-arduino-pressure-temperature-humidity/}{randomnerdtutorials.com}.

The \textbf{MPU-6050} is a six-axis MotionTracking device that integrates a 3-axis gyroscope and a 3-axis accelerometer on a single chip \cite{MPU6050_datasheet}.
It also supports both \gls{iic} and \gls{spi} communication interfaces,
with a built-in Digital Motion Processor (DMP) for advanced motion processing tasks such as sensor fusion and gesture recognition.
The device operates over a wide voltage range (2.375\,V to 3.46\,V),
making it suitable for various embedded and wearable applications.

I$^{2}$C (Inter-Integrated Circuit), originally developed by Philips Semiconductor in 1982, is a widely used synchronous serial communication protocol designed for short-distance communication between integrated circuits \cite{TI_I2C_guide}. It employs two bidirectional open-drain lines: SDA (Serial Data Line) and SCL (Serial Clock Line), both pulled high via external resistors. The protocol supports multiple masters and slaves on the same bus, with each slave device having a unique address. Communication begins with a START condition and ends with a STOP condition, allowing data to be transferred in packets of 8 bits, most significant bit (MSB) first. I$^{2}$C supports various speed modes including Standard Mode (100 kbps), Fast Mode (400 kbps), and High-Speed Mode (up to 3.4 Mbps), making it suitable for connecting microcontrollers with low-speed peripherals such as sensors and ADCs.

\subsection{Method}

The \gls{iic} protocol enables synchronous data acquisition from multiple sensors through hardware addressing. Our implementation connects two sensors: BME280 at address \texttt{0x76} and MPU6050 at address \texttt{0x68}.

Acceleration data from the MPU6050 sensor is processed using the magnitude calculation:

\begin{equation}
  a_{\text{mag}} = \sqrt{a_x^2 + a_y^2 + a_z^2}
  \label{eq:accel-mag}
\end{equation}

where \( a_x, a_y, a_z \) represent accelerations along orthogonal axes. This composite acceleration metric provides orientation-independent motion detection, ensuring reliable state identification during arbitrary device rotations (roll/pitch/yaw). As confirmed by the implementation team, this approach prevents unintended wheel stoppage when the vehicle is lifted and subjected to spatial reorientation.

The state detection system employs threshold-based validation with temporal debouncing. Suspension state is triggered when the acceleration magnitude persistently satisfies \( a_{\text{mag}} \leq 8.5\, \text{m/s}^2 \) (approximately 0.87g) for over 250ms, indicating reduced gravitational influence. Ground contact is recognized when \( a_{\text{mag}} \geq 10.0\, \text{m/s}^2 \) (approximately 1.02g) maintains for the same duration, corresponding to normal gravitational loading.

Control logic activates propulsion when suspended to maintain wheel momentum:
\begin{equation}
  \text{Control Output} = 
  \begin{cases} 
    \texttt{goStraight}(\mathit{reverse}, \mathit{baseSpeed}) & a_{\text{mag}} \leq 8.5\, \text{m/s}^2 \\ 
    \texttt{fullStop}() & a_{\text{mag}} \geq 10.0\, \text{m/s}^2 
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
with parameters configured at \(\mathit{baseSpeed} = 150\) and \(\mathit{reverse} = \mathit{false}\). This design prevents motor stall during aerial manipulation while ensuring immediate stoppage upon surface contact.

\subsection{Results}

The integrated sensor system demonstrated robust performance throughout experimental trials. Environmental parameters including temperature, humidity, and atmospheric pressure were successfully acquired via the BME280 sensor and displayed through dual-output channels: real-time serial monitoring on the host computer and sequential scrolling updates on a small display mounted on \gls{car}. This redundant visualization scheme ensured immediate parameter validation during motion operations without requiring external monitoring equipment.

\gls{car} successfully finished the test twice. In both trials, \gls{car} achieved vertical acceleration exceeding over 1g during launch phases, transitioning to suspension states at trajectory apogees. During these suspension periods, deliberate triaxial rotations were imposed through manual manipulation: roll $(\pm90^\circ)$, pitch $(\pm45^\circ)$, and yaw $(360 \text{continuous rotation})$. Throughout all rotational maneuvers, wheel propulsion maintained consistent operation at the configured base speed of 150 PWM units, with zero instances of unintended motor cutoff.
